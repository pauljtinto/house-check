## 3. ⚠️ The laneway number needs checking before you price it in

The geometry works. A 21 ft lot gives 6.4 m of lane abutment against a 3.5 m requirement, and at the maximum
10 m × 6.4 m footprint over two storeys you could reach about **1,378 sqft** — so ~1,300 is plausible on
dimensions alone.

**But Toronto also requires that a laneway suite's interior floor area be LESS than the gross floor area of
the main house.** And the main house here is listed at **1,100–1,500 sqft**.

| If main house GFA is | 1,300 sqft laneway suite |
|---|---|
| 1,100 sqft (MLS low) | **fails the rule** |
| 1,500 sqft (MLS high) | passes, barely |
| ~2,000 sqft incl. finished basement | passes |

Whether the finished basement counts toward GFA for this test is the whole question, and it is not
something I can settle from a listing. **Ask to see the laneway report and confirm it applied the
less-than-main-house constraint.** If the report sized 1,300 sqft off lot geometry alone and ignored that
rule, the real number could be several hundred square feet smaller — which changes the economics of the
single biggest upside here.

Also worth knowing: a laneway report is a feasibility opinion from a design firm, not a permit. It is a
good sign, not a guarantee.

## 4. Three things to price in

**"Newly finished" lower level — so where are the permits?** The description says newly finished, with a
separate entrance and second kitchen. Same question as 196 Brunswick: **building permit history with a
closed final inspection, plus the ESA certificate.** There is no public registry of legal second suites, so
this is the only way to know. Basement ceiling height (1.95 m minimum), egress and 30-minute fire
separation are the specific things to verify.

**The wording is hedged, and that is a tell.** The listing says *"ideal for extended family, a nanny suite
or potential income."* Not "legal second suite," not "registered." An agent with a permitted, legal
apartment says so loudly, because it is worth money. This one didn't.

**Why it matters more than anything else in this analysis:** if the suite is legal, the ~$1,800/month
counts and the effective carry is about **$8,800/month**. If it isn't, it's **$10,600/month**. That
**$1,800/month swing** is larger than every other variable here combined.

**Expect the tax bill to rise.** The $7,274 implies an MPAC assessment of roughly **$948,000 — only 63% of
the asking price**, the lowest ratio of anything we've looked at (46 Brunswick was 75%, 196 Brunswick 118%).
A newly finished basement apartment almost certainly isn't captured yet. Reassessed to a normal ratio, the
bill lands somewhere around **$671–767/month**, so budget an extra **$65–160/month**. Not fatal, but it is
the mirror image of the 196 Brunswick lesson: a low assessment on a freshly renovated house is a bill that
hasn't arrived yet.

## 5. ⚠️ The gate I can't settle from here: bikeability

This is the one place 196 Brunswick was clearly better, and the answer depends on a measurement I can't
make from a listing.

The property is "steps to Christie Pits Park and the subway," which puts it near Bloor. **Bloor is the
Bill 212 removal corridor** — the one the Court of Appeal cleared for removal on 14 August. Harbord, the
poured-concrete track that survives, runs further south along Bickford Park.

Three scenarios, run through the model:

| Your actual route | Score | If the Bloor lanes go |
|---|---|---|
| Bloor, ~200 m away | 82/100 | **49/100 — fails your threshold of 60** |
| Harbord, ~400 m | **93/100** | 93/100 — no change |
| Harbord, ~800 m | 71/100 | 71/100 — no change |

**So the question is simply: how far is it to Harbord, and would you actually ride it instead of Bloor?**
If Harbord is a 400 m roll south, this scores 93 and is as bulletproof as 196 Brunswick. If your honest
daily route is Bloor, you are buying a 33-point resilience gap into a corridor the province can now legally
rip out.

**You have an open house on Saturday. Ride there.** Time the route to Harbord, and see whether it's a
natural ride or a detour you'd stop making in February. That single test resolves the most important open
question on this property, and it costs you twenty minutes.

# Addendum — 2026-09-19, after the notables sheet and the pre-listing inspection

Two documents, and they do not say the same thing. The inspection is the real one.

**First, credit where it's due:** this is a **pre-listing inspection commissioned by the listing agent**
(National Home Inspection, Richard Gaughan R.H.I., Sept 7, client "Charlene Kalia"). Most sellers would
not hand a buyer a report that flags a $10,000+ priority item. They did. That's a good sign about who you're
dealing with — and it's also a negotiating lever, because it is *their own document*.

It is still not a substitute for your own inspection. It explicitly excludes mould, asbestos, lead paint,
radon and UFFI — all live possibilities in a **120-year-old** house.

---

## 1. The finding that matters: knob-and-tube wiring

> *"The wiring on the 2nd floor is largely original knob&tube wire and will require eventual upgrade."*
> **P: budget for replacement of remaining original wire. ($10,000+)**

"P" is the inspector's own top category — *priority repair/safety concern within the next 1 year*. It is
the only P-rated cost item in the report.

**This is bigger than a repair bill, for three reasons.**

**Insurance.** Many Canadian insurers decline to write a policy on a house with active knob-and-tube, and
those that will often require removal within 30–90 days of closing. Your lender requires insurance to fund.
So this isn't just a cost — it's a potential obstacle to closing. **Get a written insurance quote before
you remove conditions.** That is the single most important thing on this list and it costs you a phone call.

**The real number is higher than $10,000.** A second-floor rewire in a 120-year-old house with original
plaster means opening walls and ceilings, then repairing and repainting them. Budget **$15,000–25,000**.

**They upgraded around it, not through it.** The notables sheet lists *"Updated electric plugs and switches
on the main and all upper – 2022."* That's replacing devices, not rewiring. The inspection confirms the
ungrounded second-floor outlets were fitted with **GFCI devices** — which is the ESA-recommended *interim*
measure for knob-and-tube, not a fix. Somebody made it safe and legal to live with, and left the wire.

---

## 2. The 100-amp service quietly undercuts the upside

The house has **100-amp service**, already carrying a basement stove (40A), a main-floor stove (40A) and a
dryer (30A). The inspector calls it "sufficient for the electrical requirements of the house" — of *this*
house, as it stands.

The notables sheet says *"The property could easily be converted into three separate apartments."*
On 100 amps, with knob-and-tube upstairs, **"easily" is not the word.** A three-unit conversion or a
1,300 sqft laneway suite would almost certainly need a **200-amp upgrade: $3,000–6,000** on top of the
rewire.

The growth and income story here is real, but it is gated behind the electrical, and the listing materials
don't mention that.

## 3. ⚠️ The laneway number is the geometric maximum — confirmed

I flagged in §3 above that the ~1,300 sqft laneway figure might not survive Toronto's rule that a laneway
suite's interior floor area must be **less than the main house's gross floor area**. The notables sheet
gives the precise figure: **"Potential for a 2-story Laneway house up to 1377 sq ft."**

Run the envelope: a 21 ft lot is 6.40 m wide, the maximum length is 10 m, over two storeys —
**1,378 sqft.** The claimed figure is the geometric maximum to within one square foot.

That tells you the report sized the **building envelope**, not the GFA test. And the main house is listed at
**1,100–1,500 sqft**:

| Main house GFA | 1,377 sqft laneway suite |
|---|---|
| 1,100 sqft (MLS low) | **fails** |
| 1,500 sqft (MLS high) | passes, barely |
| ~2,200 sqft incl. finished basement | passes |

Whether the finished basement counts toward GFA for this test decides it. **Ask for the actual report and
check whether it addressed the constraint at all.** If it didn't, the buildable suite could be materially
smaller than 1,377 sqft — and that is the headline upside on this property.
